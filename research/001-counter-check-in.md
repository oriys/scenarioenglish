# Research — Scene 001 Counter Check-in

> Purpose: establish the research baseline for the first course lesson. This file records real-world process, current policy-sensitive facts, source material, and course-design decisions before the lesson is written.

## 1. Research question

What does a traveller actually need to understand and say when using an airline check-in counter, and which parts belong in Scene 001 rather than later airport scenes?

The lesson should train the smallest complete task:

> Arrive at the airline check-in area → present travel documents → check any hold baggage → resolve one counter-level issue → receive/confirm travel documents and baggage receipt → continue to security.

Scene 001 should **not** try to teach the whole airport journey. Security screening, finding the gate, boarding groups and missed boarding belong in later scenes.

## 2. Real-world flow

### 2.1 Finding the correct area

Heathrow states that airline check-in and bag-drop areas are shown on overhead Departures screens. After check-in and bag drop, passengers proceed to security.

Real-world learner tasks:

- find the correct airline/flight area;
- distinguish `Check-in` from `Bag Drop`;
- have passport/travel documents ready;
- know whether they need a staffed counter or only bag drop.

### 2.2 Document check

British Airways states that passengers using bag drop need their passport, required travel documents and boarding pass. At a staffed counter, staff may check identity, booking and required travel documents before accepting baggage.

Course implication:

- `passport`, `travel documents`, `booking reference`, `boarding pass` are high-value terms;
- the learner should be comfortable handing over documents and clarifying what is being requested;
- the lesson should avoid implying that a paper booking confirmation is always required.

### 2.3 Checked baggage acceptance

At the counter the operational tasks can include:

- confirming how many bags are being checked;
- placing a bag on the scale;
- checking size/weight against the applicable allowance;
- attaching a baggage tag;
- issuing a baggage receipt/identification stub;
- explaining what the passenger must remove or repack if a bag cannot be accepted as presented.

Heathrow specifically tells travellers to keep the baggage receipt.

Course implication:

`baggage tag` and `baggage receipt` should be taught as practical objects, not just vocabulary.

### 2.4 Seat preference

A staffed counter may be able to confirm or change a seat, but this is airline-, fare- and availability-dependent. It should be treated as an optional counter interaction rather than a guaranteed check-in step.

Course implication:

- seat preference appears as an optional request;
- it should not dominate Scene 001 because Scene 004 is dedicated to seat selection and changes.

### 2.5 Connecting flights and through-checked baggage

British Airways states that bags can usually be checked through to the final destination when connecting flights are on one booking reference/ticket, subject to the itinerary and airport requirements. Separate bookings normally require the passenger to collect and check the bag again.

The baggage tag shows the destination to which the bag has been checked.

Course implication:

A traveller with a connection should be able to ask:

- `Is my bag checked through to my final destination?`
- `Do I need to collect and recheck it?`

However, the lesson must not teach “you never collect your bag on a connection” as a universal rule.

### 2.6 End of the counter task

Once document/baggage handling is complete, the traveller leaves the counter and continues to security. Gate-finding and boarding are later scenes.

Course implication:

The lesson should end with a clear handoff:

- keep boarding pass / baggage receipt;
- go to security;
- do not expand into the full boarding journey.

## 3. Real-world English

### 3.1 Signs / screens

High-value recognition items:

- Departures
- Check-in
- Bag Drop
- Self-service check-in
- Economy / Business Class
- Baggage allowance
- Oversized baggage
- Security

### 3.2 Documents / objects

- passport
- travel documents
- booking reference
- boarding pass / boarding card
- checked bag / hold luggage
- cabin bag / hand luggage
- scale
- baggage tag
- baggage receipt

### 3.3 High-probability staff intentions

The exact wording varies by airline and airport. The lesson should therefore teach **intent families**, not pretend one script is universal.

High-probability intentions:

1. identify destination / flight;
2. request passport or travel documents;
3. confirm checked baggage quantity;
4. ask passenger to place bag on scale;
5. explain overweight/oversize issue;
6. ask about restricted or dangerous items when required;
7. confirm or offer a seat option if available;
8. explain whether baggage is checked through;
9. hand over boarding pass / baggage receipt;
10. direct passenger to security.

Representative natural wording:

- `Where are you flying to today?`
- `May I see your passport, please?`
- `Are you checking in any bags?`
- `How many bags are you checking?`
- `Please put your bag on the scale.`
- `Your bag is over the allowance.`
- `Would you like to take something out?`
- `Your bag is checked through to Edinburgh.`
- `You'll need to collect and recheck it in London.`
- `Here's your boarding pass and baggage receipt.`
- `Please keep the baggage receipt.`
- `You can go through security now.`

## 4. Common failure points

### 4.1 Overweight or oversized baggage

This is common enough to train, but the exact allowance and fee are airline/fare specific.

Course rule:

- teach the language for understanding the issue and asking for options;
- use fictional numbers in role-play;
- explicitly state that exercise numbers are not airline policy.

### 4.2 Power bank / spare lithium batteries in checked baggage

This is policy-sensitive and currently important.

The UK Civil Aviation Authority states that power banks must be carried in carry-on baggage, must be individually protected when not in use, and under the current UK rules a maximum of two power banks per person may be carried; higher-capacity batteries can require operator approval. The CAA also advises that spare lithium batteries and power banks should not be placed in checked baggage.

Course rule:

- Scene 001 teaches the language needed if staff identify this problem;
- the lesson does **not** ask the learner to memorise all watt-hour thresholds;
- the learner should understand `power bank`, `spare battery`, `checked bag`, `cabin bag`, `take it out`;
- because rules vary by jurisdiction/operator and can change, the lesson tells the learner to check the current airline/airport rules.

### 4.3 Connection baggage assumption

A learner may assume that a checked bag always goes to the final destination.

Course rule:

Teach explicit confirmation rather than assumption:

- check the baggage tag destination;
- ask whether collection/recheck is required.

### 4.4 Seat unavailable

Counter staff may not be able to provide the preferred seat.

Course rule:

Treat this as a light optional problem; deeper seat-change language belongs in Scene 004.

### 4.5 Late arrival / closed check-in

Check-in closing times vary by airline, airport and route.

Course rule:

- mention that deadlines exist;
- never publish one universal cutoff time;
- detailed missed-check-in/boarding recovery belongs in later disruption scenes.

## 5. Current rules / policy-sensitive facts

Facts intentionally kept narrow in the lesson:

- airline check-in/bag-drop location is typically shown in Departures signage/screens;
- travel documents may be inspected at check-in/bag drop;
- checked baggage is weighed/tagged and the passenger should keep the baggage receipt;
- through-checking depends on booking/itinerary/airport rules;
- power banks and spare lithium batteries must not be treated as normal checked-baggage items; current UK CAA guidance requires power banks in carry-on baggage and imposes specific limits/conditions;
- baggage allowance, fees, seat availability and check-in deadlines are **not universal**.

## 7. Course design decisions

### Keep in Scene 001

- finding the staffed check-in area;
- document handover;
- checked-bag quantity and weighing;
- baggage tag / receipt;
- one lightweight seat request;
- overweight-bag recovery;
- power-bank/spare-battery correction language;
- connection baggage confirmation;
- leaving for security;
- conversation repair for numbers and unclear instructions.

### Move out of Scene 001

- detailed self-service kiosk flow → Scene 002;
- oversized/special baggage depth → Scene 003;
- seat-change depth → Scene 004;
- connection strategy and tight connection → Scene 005 / later disruption scenes;
- security screening → Scenes 006–009;
- gate finding → Scene 012;
- boarding groups → Scene 013;
- missed boarding → Scene 016.

## 8. What we intentionally do NOT teach

To keep the first lesson useful and reusable, it will not require memorising:

- a specific airline's baggage allowance;
- a specific excess-baggage fee;
- a universal check-in closing time;
- all lithium-battery watt-hour thresholds;
- every possible dangerous-good category;
- detailed boarding/gate procedures;
- a single “correct” staff script.

The learner should instead leave Scene 001 able to:

1. understand the intent of common counter questions;
2. answer with short, natural English;
3. confirm critical information;
4. resolve a basic baggage problem;
5. know when to ask rather than assume.

## 9. Quality bar for the rewritten lesson

The rewritten Scene 001 should be accepted only if it:

- reflects the researched task boundary;
- uses current policy notes only where necessary;
- distinguishes active vocabulary from recognition vocabulary;
- introduces no more than five global Patterns;
- uses three shorter role plays instead of one monolithic script;
- marks exercise-only numbers as fictional;
- trains baggage receipt/tag and connection confirmation;
- trains power-bank correction without becoming a dangerous-goods lecture;
- ends at the handoff to security.
