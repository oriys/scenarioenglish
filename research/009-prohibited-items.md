# Research — Scene 009 Prohibited Items & Confiscation

> This note covers the fourth and last scene of the security sub-theme: what happens when something in hand baggage is **not allowed** — the sorts of items that get pulled, and the practical options a passenger has (return to check-in, surrender, or recover later). This closes the 006–009 block.

## 1. Research question

What does a traveller need to understand and say when security tells them an item is prohibited in hand baggage, and what are the realistic options for dealing with it?

The lesson should train the smallest usable task:

> Officer identifies a non-allowed item → passenger understands the problem → asks/confirms their options (can it be checked in the hold? should it be surrendered/discarded? can it be posted or collected later?) → makes a decision → either repacks via check-in or lets the item be confiscated → continues to the gate.

Scene 009 trains **recognition of common prohibited item types** and **the options language** — not a memorised nationwide list.

## 2. Real-world flow

### 2.1 Which items commonly cause confiscation in hand baggage

Commonly pulled at UK security (Heathrow prep list; gov.uk personal-items table; CAA dangerous-goods tables):

- **Liquids over the applicable limit** (over 100ml at most airports; over 2L containers even where the cap is raised) and liquids not conforming to a clear-bag requirement where that applies.
- **Sharp objects:**
  - knives with a sharp/pointed blade and/or blade longer than **6cm** — banned in cabin (allowed in hold, check with airline);
  - large scissors (blades over **6cm**) — banned in cabin;
  - razor blades (box cutters), axes, ice axes, swords, martial-arts blades.
  - Small scissors (≤6cm), round-ended/blunt scissors, nail clippers, tweezers, a simple corkscrew (handle on that: gov.uk says corkscrew is "No / Yes" i.e. banned in hand, allowed in hold) are fine.
- **Sports equipment / blunt items:** baseball/softball/cricket bats, golf clubs, clubs/batons, martial-arts striking equipment — cabin banned, hold allowed (check with airline).
- **Dangerous-goods type items:** lighter fuel, flammable gases/liquids, corrosives, fireworks, non-safety matches, wet-cell car batteries (banned both cabin and hold).
- **Power banks / spare lithium batteries above allowance** (see 007) — e.g. more than two, or over the approved capacity, or being carried in the wrong bag.
- **Duty-free over-limit liquids** bought airside but stopped because the STEB (security tamper-evident bag) is broken or the destination restricts liquids (e.g. some routes apply different rules).

### 2.2 When an item is found — the options

Real-world disposition options offered by airport/security staff and airline staff (this is where realistic course content lives). Common phrasing of the three choices:

1. **Return to check-in** — if there's still time, the passenger can go back to the airline desk and **put the item in hold baggage** (if the item is allowed in hold). The passenger must leave the secured area / go back out to the check-in desk.
2. **Surrender / dispose** — the passenger hands the item over / places it in a disposal bin and it is confiscated/destroyed (liquids being the most common).
3. **Post / recover later** — some airports and third-party services (e.g. excess-baggage companies) offer to **post prohibited items back** or store them for collection within a window (commonly ~30 days) for a fee. Not every airport offers this; the officer or desk staff will say if it's an option.

Practical teaching point: the *officer or airline staff will usually state the option(s)*; the passenger's job is to understand and choose (e.g. "Can I put this in my checked bag?" / "I'll just leave it, throw it away, then.").

Note that whether an item is allowed in **hold** at all matters — a sharp knife can go in hold, but lighter fuel or a power bank cannot go in hold either. So "return to check-in" only works for hold-permitted items.

### 2.3 Language: how officers broach it

Officers usually state the item and name it, then give a choice or ask for confirmation. Learners should be able to:

- recognise the item name (knife, scissors, bottle, lighter, power bank);
- understand "this isn't allowed in hand baggage";
- ask whether it can go in the hold / checked luggage;
- say whether they want to surrender it (dispose) or take it back to check-in;
- confirm politely and not argue aggressively (the item cannot board the aircraft in hand baggage).

## 3. Real-world English

### 3.1 Recognition vocabulary (item groups)

- liquids above the limit / over-size container
- knife / scissors / razor blade / sharp object
- sports equipment (bat / golf clubs) / blunt item
- lighter fuel / flammable / corrosive / fireworks (dangerous goods)
- power bank / spare battery (over allowance)
- duty-free bag / STEB (security bag) / broken seal
- hold baggage / checked baggage / cabin / hand baggage

### 3.2 High-probability staff intentions & phrasing

1. name the item and say it's not allowed;
2. ask who the item belongs to / confirm it's theirs;
3. offer or state options (check it in, surrender, post);
4. ask the passenger to confirm a decision;
5. direct them back to check-in if they choose to check it.

Representative natural wording:

- `I'm sorry, but this knife isn't allowed in hand baggage.`
- `This bottle is over 100ml, so it can't go through security.`
- `You have three choices — I can take it, you can check it in, or you can get it sent home.`
- `Would you like to (check it in / leave it with us)?`
- `If you want to check it in, you'll need to go back to your airline's desk.`
- `It's not allowed in hold baggage either, I'm afraid.`

Learner output prompts:

- `Can I put it in my checked luggage instead?` (P004/P057)
- `Can I go back and check it in?` (P004)
- `OK, then you can take it / just throw it away.` (decision)
- `What are my options?` (P065 / P003)
- `Sorry, did you say I need to go to my airline's desk?` (repair)

## 4. Common failure points

### 4.1 Over-limit liquid at a checkpoint

The single most common confiscation. Learners often don't know they can *sometimes* return to check-in and put it in hold. Train the question.

### 4.2 Not knowing whether hold is even an option

A sharp knife can go in hold; lighter fuel, most flammables and (usually) extra power banks cannot. Learners should understand the officer will say, and that "hold" is not a universal solution.

### 4.3 Arguing or pleading

Real-world guidance is that the item cannot go through in hand baggage; arguing is unlikely to help. Train calm confirmation and choosing an option.

### 4.4 Assumption that breaking a duty-free STEB bag is fine

If the security bag is opened/broken, airside-purchased liquids may be stopped, and some destinations restrict liquids. Worth a light touch so learners understand "keep the STEB sealed."

## 5. Current rules / policy-sensitive facts

`policy_sensitive: true`

`last_verified: 2026-08`

Key stable language/facts to teach (but teach as item-types + "ask/confirm", not an exhaustive permanent list):

- **Knife / sharp blade over 6cm** is banned in cabin, allowed in hold (check with airline); small scissors ≤6cm and blunt scissors are fine in cabin. This 6cm rule is stable UK policy (gov.uk + Heathrow agree).
- **Sports bats/clubs** (baseball, cricket, golf clubs) are banned in cabin, allowed in hold with airline confirmation.
- **Dangerous goods** (lighter fuel, flammables, corrosives, fireworks, non-safety matches, wet-cell car batteries) are banned in both cabin and hold.
- **Power banks / spare lithium batteries:** hand/cabin only, individually protected, max two per person, ≤100 Wh (100–160 Wh needs airline approval); never in checked baggage (see 007/CAA table).
- **Liquids**: cap is airport-specific (100ml most places; 2L at several major airports as of early 2026) and **still changing** — do not teach one number across the UK.
- Confiscated-item **options** (return-to-check-in / check in the hold, surrender/dispose, or post/recover later) exist at many airports but availability and fees differ; the officer/airline staff state what's on offer.

## 7. Course design decisions

### Keep in Scene 009

- recognising and naming common prohibited item groups (sharp >6cm, over-limit liquids, sports bats/clubs, dangerous-goods, battery items);
- the "this isn't allowed in hand baggage" moment and staying calm;
- asking the options questions (`Can I put it in my checked luggage?`, `What are my options?`, `Can I go back and check it in?`);
- choosing between return-to-check-in, surrender, or post-later when offered;
- understanding that hold is not always an option (dangerous goods, batteries);
- the duty-free STEB "keep it sealed" note.

### Move out of Scene 009

- sign/queue navigation → 006;
- liquid/electronics limits and power-bank basics → 007;
- manual search procedure → 008.

### Reusable patterns (PATTERN_BANK.md)

- P004 — `Can I + action?`

## 8. What we intentionally do NOT teach

To keep Scene 009 useful and honest, it will NOT require memorising:

- an exhaustive UK-wide list of permitted/banned items (it's long and changes);
- a single UK liquid number (see 007);
- watt-hour thresholds for every battery type (identification only; scene 007 covers the direction);
- the fees or exact 30-day window of post/recover services as if universal;
- any implication that a confiscation is a criminal matter — it is an operational security decision.

The learner should instead leave Scene 009 able to:

1. recognise and name the common item groups that get stopped;
2. understand "this isn't allowed in hand baggage";
3. ask about checking the item in the hold;
4. make a calm decision (check it in / surrender / have it sent back) using short natural English;
5. know the duty-free security-bag rule and re-check rules.
